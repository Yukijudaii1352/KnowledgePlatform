<div class="ltx_abstract">
<h6 class="ltx_title ltx_title_abstract">Abstract</h6>
<p id="id20.id1" class="ltx_p">The grand aim of having a single robot that can manipulate arbitrary objects in diverse settings is at odds with the paucity of robotics datasets. Acquiring and growing such datasets is strenuous due to manual efforts, operational costs, and safety challenges. A path toward such an universal agent would require a structured framework capable of wide generalization but trained within a reasonable data budget.
In this paper, we develop an efficient system (<span id="id20.id1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">RoboAgent</span>) for training universal agents capable of multi-task manipulation skills using (a) <span id="id20.id1.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">semantic augmentations</span> that can rapidly multiply existing datasets and (b) <span id="id20.id1.3" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">action representations</span> that can extract performant policies with small yet diverse multi-modal datasets without overfitting. In addition, reliable task conditioning and an expressive policy architecture enable our agent to exhibit a diverse repertoire of skills in novel situations specified using language commands. Using merely 7500 demonstrations, we are able to train a single agent capable of 12 unique skills, and demonstrate its generalization over 38 tasks spread across common daily activities in diverse kitchen scenes. On average, <span id="id20.id1.4" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">RoboAgent</span>  outperforms prior methods by over 40% in unseen situations while being more sample efficient and being amenable to capability improvements and extensions through fine-tuning.</p>
</div>

<section id="S1" class="ltx_section">
<h2 class="ltx_title ltx_title_section">
<span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_section">1 </span>Introduction</h2>

<div id="S1.p1" class="ltx_para ltx_noindent">
<p id="S1.p1.1" class="ltx_p">Training a robot manipulator with multiple skills requires exposure to diverse experiences and the ability to acquire skills from a diverse data corpus.
To collect such a multi-skill data corpus in the real world requires substantial effort and suffers from high operational costs and safety challenges. Given the expense, efficiency in robot learning paradigms is necessary for real-world training and deployment. While there are recent efforts in scaling real-world robotic datasets despite these challenges <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib1" title="" class="ltx_ref">1</a>, <a href="#bib.bib2" title="" class="ltx_ref">2</a>, <a href="#bib.bib3" title="" class="ltx_ref">3</a>]</cite>, efficiency seems to be overlooked in the attempts to scale <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib4" title="" class="ltx_ref">4</a>, <a href="#bib.bib5" title="" class="ltx_ref">5</a>, <a href="#bib.bib6" title="" class="ltx_ref">6</a>, <a href="#bib.bib7" title="" class="ltx_ref">7</a>]</cite>.</p>
</div>
<div id="S1.p2" class="ltx_para ltx_noindent">
<p id="S1.p2.1" class="ltx_p">With the acknowledgment that robot learning will generally benefit as the scale of the robotics dataset grows, the focus of our work is on investigating generalization in developing capable agents under a <em id="S1.p2.1.1" class="ltx_emph ltx_font_italic">given data budget</em>. We restrict ourselves to a dataset with 7,500 robot manipulation trajectories (an order of magnitude less than related works <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib5" title="" class="ltx_ref">5</a>]</cite>) containing a diverse collection of manipulation skills across different tasks. As a robot under deployment in real environments like homes, hospitals, etc., will always find itself in unseen scenarios, we set out to develop the most capable agent with an emphasis on its <span id="S1.p2.1.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">ability to generalize to novel situations within this data budget</span>.</p>
</div>
<div id="S1.p3" class="ltx_para ltx_noindent">
<p id="S1.p3.1" class="ltx_p">At first sight, wide generalization with a data budget seems like wishful thinking - while it’s possible to provide large representation capabilities to the agent’s policy, scaling without data diversity will likely lead to overfitting and no generalization. Our insight is twofold: (1) We ensure sufficient coverage of different skills in different scenarios in a dataset (of 7500 trajectories) we collect through teleoperation. The collected dataset is diversified offline, at no extra human/robot cost, via semantic augmentations  <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib8" title="" class="ltx_ref">8</a>, <a href="#bib.bib9" title="" class="ltx_ref">9</a>, <a href="#bib.bib10" title="" class="ltx_ref">10</a>]</cite> to aid generalization in novel situations. (2) We train a language-conditioned manipulation policy with <span id="S1.p3.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">MT-ACT</span> – multi-task action-chunking transformers capable of handling the multi-modal data distribution. The architecture leverages the fact that robot movements are temporally correlated, by predicting action chunks <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib11" title="" class="ltx_ref">11</a>]</cite> instead of per-step actions, leading to smoother behaviors and mitigation of covariate shift commonly observed in the low data imitation learning regime.</p>
</div>
<div id="S1.p4" class="ltx_para ltx_noindent">
<p id="S1.p4.1" class="ltx_p">Overall, we emphasize that the data efficiency lessons we present are <em id="S1.p4.1.1" class="ltx_emph ltx_font_italic">general</em> and will help in achieving generalizable agents independent of the available data budget. Building on these insights, we make the following contributions:</p>
<ul id="S1.I1" class="ltx_itemize">
<li id="S1.I1.i1" class="ltx_item" style="list-style-type:none;">
<span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_item">•</span> 
<div id="S1.I1.i1.p1" class="ltx_para ltx_noindent">
<p id="S1.I1.i1.p1.1" class="ltx_p">We present an efficient method <span id="S1.I1.i1.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text">MT-ACT</span> designed to recover <span id="S1.I1.i1.p1.1.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">generalist agents on a data budget</span>. <span id="S1.I1.i1.p1.1.3" class="ltx_text">MT-ACT</span> leverages data multiplication via semantic augmentations and action representations to drive efficiency gains in low-data settings.</p>
</div>
</li>
<li id="S1.I1.i2" class="ltx_item" style="list-style-type:none;">
<span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_item">•</span> 
<div id="S1.I1.i2.p1" class="ltx_para ltx_noindent">
<p id="S1.I1.i2.p1.1" class="ltx_p"><span id="S1.I1.i2.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text">MT-ACT</span>’s architecture can effectively ingest multi-modal trajectory data to recover <span id="S1.I1.i2.p1.1.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">RoboAgent</span>  – a single policy that can perform a diverse set of tasks through language instructions. Through extensive real-world experiments, we show <span id="S1.I1.i2.p1.1.3" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">RoboAgent</span>  is <span id="S1.I1.i2.p1.1.4" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">capable of exhibiting 12 manipulation skills</span>.</p>
</div>
</li>
<li id="S1.I1.i3" class="ltx_item" style="list-style-type:none;">
<span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_item">•</span> 
<div id="S1.I1.i3.p1" class="ltx_para ltx_noindent">
<p id="S1.I1.i3.p1.1" class="ltx_p">We perform extensive generalization studies to demonstrate that <span id="S1.I1.i3.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text">MT-ACT</span> is 40 <math id="S1.I1.i3.p1.1.m1.1" class="ltx_Math" alttext="\%" display="inline"><semantics id="S1.I1.i3.p1.1.m1.1a"><mo id="S1.I1.i3.p1.1.m1.1.1" xref="S1.I1.i3.p1.1.m1.1.1.cmml">%</mo><annotation-xml encoding="MathML-Content" id="S1.I1.i3.p1.1.m1.1b"><csymbol cd="latexml" id="S1.I1.i3.p1.1.m1.1.1.cmml" xref="S1.I1.i3.p1.1.m1.1.1">percent</csymbol></annotation-xml><annotation encoding="application/x-tex" id="S1.I1.i3.p1.1.m1.1c">\%</annotation></semantics></math> more performant than alternatives, exhibits much <span id="S1.I1.i3.p1.1.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">superior generalization to diverse novel scenarios</span>, and is amenable to <span id="S1.I1.i3.p1.1.3" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">improvements and extensions during deployment through fine-tuning</span>.</p>
</div>
</li>
<li id="S1.I1.i4" class="ltx_item" style="list-style-type:none;">
<span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_item">•</span> 
<div id="S1.I1.i4.p1" class="ltx_para ltx_noindent">
<p id="S1.I1.i4.p1.1" class="ltx_p">We meticulously recorded all the data collected during the course of the project which we are open-sourcing as part of <span id="S1.I1.i4.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_typewriter">RoboSet</span>  - one of the <span id="S1.I1.i4.p1.1.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">largest open-source robotics dataset</span> on commodity hardware. It contains high-quality human teleOp trajectories spanning a balanced distribution of 12 skills across 38 tasks in diverse kitchen scenes.</p>
</div>
</li>
</ul>
</div>

<section id="S2" class="ltx_section">
<h2 class="ltx_title ltx_title_section">
<span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_section">2 </span>Related Work</h2>

<figure id="S2.F2" class="ltx_figure"><img src="/html/2309.01918/assets/x2.png" id="S2.F2.g1" class="ltx_graphics ltx_centering ltx_img_landscape" width="328" height="150" alt="Refer to caption">
<figcaption class="ltx_caption ltx_centering"><span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_figure">Figure 2: </span>Two stage framework: [Left] <span id="S2.F2.4.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Semantic augmentation</span> stage diversifies the robot data offline using inpainting augmentations at no extra human/robot cost. [Right] <span id="S2.F2.5.2" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Policy learning</span> stage trains language-conditioned policy using <span id="S2.F2.6.3" class="ltx_text">MT-ACT</span> – multi-task action-chunking transformers – which leverages efficient action representations for ingesting multi-modal multi-task data into a single multi-skill multi-task policy.</figcaption>
</figure>
<div id="S2.p1" class="ltx_para ltx_noindent">
<p id="S2.p1.1" class="ltx_p"><span id="S2.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Frameworks for Scaling Robot Learning.</span> Given the cost of supervision in robot learning, self-supervised learning <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib12" title="" class="ltx_ref">12</a>, <a href="#bib.bib13" title="" class="ltx_ref">13</a>, <a href="#bib.bib14" title="" class="ltx_ref">14</a>]</cite> methods leveraging large unlabeled datasets have been a dominant paradigm in efforts towards building general-purpose agents.
Large-scale simulations <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib15" title="" class="ltx_ref">15</a>, <a href="#bib.bib16" title="" class="ltx_ref">16</a>, <a href="#bib.bib17" title="" class="ltx_ref">17</a>, <a href="#bib.bib18" title="" class="ltx_ref">18</a>]</cite> have also been leveraged with the hope of learning a general multi-task policy for diverse tasks <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib19" title="" class="ltx_ref">19</a>, <a href="#bib.bib20" title="" class="ltx_ref">20</a>, <a href="#bib.bib21" title="" class="ltx_ref">21</a>, <a href="#bib.bib22" title="" class="ltx_ref">22</a>, <a href="#bib.bib23" title="" class="ltx_ref">23</a>, <a href="#bib.bib24" title="" class="ltx_ref">24</a>]</cite> first and then transferring it to the real world via sim2real<cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib25" title="" class="ltx_ref">25</a>, <a href="#bib.bib26" title="" class="ltx_ref">26</a>, <a href="#bib.bib27" title="" class="ltx_ref">27</a>, <a href="#bib.bib28" title="" class="ltx_ref">28</a>]</cite>.
However, many existing multi-task RL works focus on narrow domains in simulation <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib22" title="" class="ltx_ref">22</a>, <a href="#bib.bib29" title="" class="ltx_ref">29</a>]</cite>, and those in the real-world show limited generalization and task diversity <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib30" title="" class="ltx_ref">30</a>, <a href="#bib.bib9" title="" class="ltx_ref">9</a>]</cite>.
While other works <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib19" title="" class="ltx_ref">19</a>, <a href="#bib.bib20" title="" class="ltx_ref">20</a>, <a href="#bib.bib31" title="" class="ltx_ref">31</a>]</cite> focus on multi-task settings in diverse scenarios, they restrict to evaluating trained policies mostly in simulation. By contrast, our work focuses on a large, diverse set of real-world manipulation tasks. Recently, many works have used imitation learning with large-scale real-world robot tele-operation datasets of high quality <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib2" title="" class="ltx_ref">2</a>, <a href="#bib.bib3" title="" class="ltx_ref">3</a>, <a href="#bib.bib32" title="" class="ltx_ref">32</a>, <a href="#bib.bib1" title="" class="ltx_ref">1</a>, <a href="#bib.bib33" title="" class="ltx_ref">33</a>, <a href="#bib.bib34" title="" class="ltx_ref">34</a>]</cite> .
While early works collect limited real-world data <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib3" title="" class="ltx_ref">3</a>, <a href="#bib.bib33" title="" class="ltx_ref">33</a>]</cite>,
more recent approaches <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib1" title="" class="ltx_ref">1</a>, <a href="#bib.bib5" title="" class="ltx_ref">5</a>, <a href="#bib.bib7" title="" class="ltx_ref">7</a>]</cite> collect much larger datasets. In fact, <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib5" title="" class="ltx_ref">5</a>]</cite> gathers, possibly, the largest dataset (<math id="S2.p1.1.m1.1" class="ltx_Math" alttext="\approx 130K" display="inline"><semantics id="S2.p1.1.m1.1a"><mrow id="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1" xref="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.cmml"><mi id="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.2" xref="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.2.cmml"></mi><mo id="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.1" xref="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.1.cmml">≈</mo><mrow id="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.3" xref="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.3.cmml"><mn id="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.3.2" xref="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.3.2.cmml">130</mn><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em" id="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.3.1" xref="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.3.1.cmml">​</mo><mi id="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.3.3" xref="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.3.3.cmml">K</mi></mrow></mrow><annotation-xml encoding="MathML-Content" id="S2.p1.1.m1.1b"><apply id="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.cmml" xref="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1"><approx id="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.1.cmml" xref="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.1"></approx><csymbol cd="latexml" id="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.2.cmml" xref="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.2">absent</csymbol><apply id="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.3.cmml" xref="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.3"><times id="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.3.1.cmml" xref="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.3.1"></times><cn type="integer" id="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.3.2.cmml" xref="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.3.2">130</cn><ci id="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.3.3.cmml" xref="S2.p1.1.m1.1.1.3.3">𝐾</ci></apply></apply></annotation-xml><annotation encoding="application/x-tex" id="S2.p1.1.m1.1c">\approx 130K</annotation></semantics></math> demonstrations) outside bin and place settings and shows impressive generalization with skills learned using this data.
Our work is similar in spirit, <em id="S2.p1.1.2" class="ltx_emph ltx_font_italic">i.e.</em>, we focus on real-world manipulation tasks and aim to learn a multi-task policy using <em id="S2.p1.1.3" class="ltx_emph ltx_font_italic">limited</em> real-world demonstrations.
However, unlike <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib1" title="" class="ltx_ref">1</a>]</cite>, we avoid toy environment setups and focus on realistic real-world kitchen setups with clutter and multiple feasible tasks in a scene.
Additionally, our agents exhibit a much greater diversity of skills than <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib5" title="" class="ltx_ref">5</a>, <a href="#bib.bib7" title="" class="ltx_ref">7</a>, <a href="#bib.bib28" title="" class="ltx_ref">28</a>]</cite> while being trained only on 7.5k trajectories, as opposed to 135k in <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib5" title="" class="ltx_ref">5</a>]</cite>. Importantly, we collect our data with commodity hardware (see <a href="#S4.F6" title="Figure 6 ‣ 4 Experimental Design ‣ RoboAgent: Generalization and Efficiency in Robot Manipulation via Semantic Augmentations and Action Chunking" class="ltx_ref ltx_refmacro_autoref"><span class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Figure 6</span></a>) and are making it readily available to robotic researchers worldwide.</p>
</div>
<div id="S2.p2" class="ltx_para ltx_noindent">
<p id="S2.p2.1" class="ltx_p"><span id="S2.p2.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Alternate Data Sources in Robotics.</span>
Recent successes of large-scale self-supervised approaches within both language and vision communities have showcased the advantage of large-scale data. Many recent works propose using pre-trained visual representations trained primarily on non-robot datasets <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib35" title="" class="ltx_ref">35</a>, <a href="#bib.bib36" title="" class="ltx_ref">36</a>]</cite>, for learning control policies <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib37" title="" class="ltx_ref">37</a>, <a href="#bib.bib38" title="" class="ltx_ref">38</a>, <a href="#bib.bib26" title="" class="ltx_ref">26</a>, <a href="#bib.bib39" title="" class="ltx_ref">39</a>, <a href="#bib.bib40" title="" class="ltx_ref">40</a>]</cite>. Most of these works focus on single-task settings <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib37" title="" class="ltx_ref">37</a>, <a href="#bib.bib38" title="" class="ltx_ref">38</a>, <a href="#bib.bib41" title="" class="ltx_ref">41</a>, <a href="#bib.bib42" title="" class="ltx_ref">42</a>]</cite>, or in simulated robot environments <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib26" title="" class="ltx_ref">26</a>, <a href="#bib.bib39" title="" class="ltx_ref">39</a>]</cite>.
Given the inherently large cost of collecting real-world robotics datasets, many works have focused on using alternate data sources such as language <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib43" title="" class="ltx_ref">43</a>, <a href="#bib.bib44" title="" class="ltx_ref">44</a>, <a href="#bib.bib45" title="" class="ltx_ref">45</a>, <a href="#bib.bib46" title="" class="ltx_ref">46</a>]</cite>, human videos <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib47" title="" class="ltx_ref">47</a>, <a href="#bib.bib48" title="" class="ltx_ref">48</a>, <a href="#bib.bib49" title="" class="ltx_ref">49</a>, <a href="#bib.bib50" title="" class="ltx_ref">50</a>, <a href="#bib.bib51" title="" class="ltx_ref">51</a>, <a href="#bib.bib52" title="" class="ltx_ref">52</a>, <a href="#bib.bib53" title="" class="ltx_ref">53</a>, <a href="#bib.bib54" title="" class="ltx_ref">54</a>]</cite>, and generative augmentations <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib55" title="" class="ltx_ref">55</a>, <a href="#bib.bib56" title="" class="ltx_ref">56</a>, <a href="#bib.bib9" title="" class="ltx_ref">9</a>, <a href="#bib.bib8" title="" class="ltx_ref">8</a>, <a href="#bib.bib10" title="" class="ltx_ref">10</a>]</cite>.
Our work is most similar to the latter set of works, some of which use diffusion models to generate augmentations for data collected in the real world.
However, unlike some prior works <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib9" title="" class="ltx_ref">9</a>, <a href="#bib.bib8" title="" class="ltx_ref">8</a>]</cite> our approach is fully automatic. We do not need segmentation masks <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib9" title="" class="ltx_ref">9</a>]</cite> or object meshes <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib8" title="" class="ltx_ref">8</a>]</cite> for generating augmentation data. Overall, our work is most similar to <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib10" title="" class="ltx_ref">10</a>]</cite> which adapts a pre-trained open-world object detection model <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib57" title="" class="ltx_ref">57</a>]</cite> for generating segmentations that are used with text-guided diffusion models to generate augmentations. However, our approach does not require any further fine-tuning of a separate module for open-vocabulary segmentation and language grounding. More importantly, we further investigate scaling laws with respect to semantic data augmentations to demonstrate the favorable impact of augmentations in aiding test-time generalization to unseen scenarios.</p>
</div>
</section>
<section id="S3" class="ltx_section">
<h2 class="ltx_title ltx_title_section">
<span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_section">3 </span><span id="S3.1.1" class="ltx_text">MT-ACT</span>: Multi-Task Action Chunking Transformer</h2>

<div id="S3.p1" class="ltx_para ltx_noindent">
<p id="S3.p1.1" class="ltx_p">To learn generalizable manipulation policies, it is essential for robots to be exposed to rich and diverse experiences, encompassing a wide range of skills and contextual variations.
However, operational costs and real-world challenges in collecting such extensive datasets pose a practical limit on their overall size. Our goal is to address these limitations by developing <span id="S3.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">a paradigm that can learn effective multi-task agents under a limited data budget</span>.
Our approach consists of two stages (<a href="#S2.F2" title="Figure 2 ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ RoboAgent: Generalization and Efficiency in Robot Manipulation via Semantic Augmentations and Action Chunking" class="ltx_ref ltx_refmacro_autoref"><span class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Figure 2</span></a>):</p>
</div>
<div id="S3.p2" class="ltx_para ltx_noindent">
<p id="S3.p2.1" class="ltx_p"><span id="S3.p2.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Semantic Augmentation</span> – the first stage multiplies the pre-collected dataset by creating a diverse collection of semantic augmentations over the existing robot’s experiences. These semantic augmentations recreate a particular robot demonstration into several demonstrations, each with a different semantic context (objects, textures, backgrounds, etc), at no extra robot or human cost. Such data diversification incorporates real-world semantic priors into the multi-task manipulation agents preparing them to account for out-of-distribution scenarios they might encounter during deployment.</p>
</div>
<div id="S3.p3" class="ltx_para ltx_noindent">
<p id="S3.p3.1" class="ltx_p"><span id="S3.p3.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Policy Learning</span> – the second stage learns robust skills from limited skill data, adapting design choices from previously limited single-task settings to the context of large-scale generalization in multi-task multi-scene manipulation tasks with diverse skills.
We develop <span id="S3.p3.1.2" class="ltx_text">MT-ACT</span> – a language-conditioned novel policy architecture to train robust agents capable of recovering multiple skills from multi-modal datasets. To model the diverse multi-modal multi-task augmented datasets, we employ a Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE) <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib58" title="" class="ltx_ref">58</a>]</cite> to identify action distribution modes. This enables us to fit a high-capacity Transformer <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib59" title="" class="ltx_ref">59</a>]</cite> conditioned on the CVAE encodings, effectively capturing the variations and dependencies in the augmented dataset. Our policy also leverages the fact that robot movements are temporally correlated. Predicting action chunks <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib11" title="" class="ltx_ref">11</a>]</cite> instead of per-step actions, leads to smoother behaviors and mitigation of covariance shift commonly observed in the low data imitation learning regime. Next, we outline the individual components of our method in detail.</p>
</div>

<div id="S3.SS1.p1" class="ltx_para ltx_noindent">
<p id="S3.SS1.p1.1" class="ltx_p">Training a general agent capable of robustly exhibiting a diverse repertoire of skills in novel scenes and tasks needs exposure to experiences matching this diversity. To align with our goal of building a data-efficient robot learning paradigm, we restrict ourselves to a frozen pre-collected small but diverse dataset – <span id="S3.SS1.p1.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_typewriter">RoboSet</span>(<span id="S3.SS1.p1.1.2" class="ltx_text">MT-ACT</span>).
In order to capture behavioral diversity, we ensure sufficient coverage over different core skills, where each skill if defined as a temporally correlated sequence of actions that lead to plausible change in an object’s pose. Example skills include <span id="S3.SS1.p1.1.3" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">closing/opening</span> articulated objects, <span id="S3.SS1.p1.1.4" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">sliding</span>, <span id="S3.SS1.p1.1.5" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">wiping</span>.
Each skill is instantiated across a set of objects. We refer to such (skill, object) combinations as a <span id="S3.SS1.p1.1.6" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">task</span>. Our tasks are instantiated in different kitchen scenes, visually illustrated in <a href="#A1" title="Appendix A Dataset details ‣ RoboAgent: Generalization and Efficiency in Robot Manipulation via Semantic Augmentations and Action Chunking" class="ltx_ref ltx_refmacro_autoref"><span class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">Appendix A</span></a>. Instead of a random collection of tasks, we structure groups of tasks as belonging to be part of a household <span id="S3.SS1.p1.1.7" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">activity</span>, such that they can be executed in sequence to obtain a meaningful outcome, such as cleaning a kitchen.
Further details on the different skills, tasks and activities in <span id="S3.SS1.p1.1.8" class="ltx_text ltx_font_typewriter">RoboSet</span> are provided in Appendix <a href="#A1.SS1" title="A.1 Dataset Terminology ‣ Appendix A Dataset details ‣ RoboAgent: Generalization and Efficiency in Robot Manipulation via Semantic Augmentations and Action Chunking" class="ltx_ref"><span class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">A.1</span></a>.</p>

<section id="S3.SS2" class="ltx_subsection">
<h3 class="ltx_title ltx_title_subsection">
<span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_subsection">3.2 </span>Data Augmentation</h3>

<figure id="S3.F4" class="ltx_figure"><img src="/html/2309.01918/assets/x4.png" id="S3.F4.g1" class="ltx_graphics ltx_centering ltx_img_landscape" width="456" height="160" alt="Refer to caption">
<figcaption class="ltx_caption ltx_centering"><span class="ltx_tag ltx_tag_figure">Figure 4: </span>Illustration of the data augmentations that we develop to rapidly multiply limited robot datasets with diverse semantic scene variations. In (a) we show the scene around the robot and the interaction object changing. In (b), we show the interaction object itself changing while preserving the rest of the scene.</figcaption>
</figure>
<div id="S3.SS2.p1" class="ltx_para ltx_noindent">
<p id="S3.SS2.p1.1" class="ltx_p">Generally useful robot manipulation systems will need to be able to deal with out-of-distribution scenarios (e.g. different homes and offices). Since any dataset of a practical size will have a limited diversity of objects and scenes (due to physical access and operational constraints) compared to what agents will encounter during deployment. To enable test-time generalization to the unseen scenarios, we develop a <em id="S3.SS2.p1.1.1" class="ltx_emph ltx_font_italic">fully automatic</em> offline process to multiply the dataset.</p>
</div>
<div id="S3.SS2.p2" class="ltx_para ltx_noindent">
<p id="S3.SS2.p2.1" class="ltx_p">Given an initial dataset of robot behaviors, we multiply the dataset by creating multiple semantic variations of the dataset while preserving the <span id="S3.SS2.p2.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">robot behavior</span> within each trajectory. These semantic variations are created by applying augmentations per frame within the trajectory. Augmentations are created by inpainting a part of the image frame introducing new objects and scene variations. The inpainting locations are specified by a mask and are informed by a text prompt. As opposed to <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib9" title="" class="ltx_ref">9</a>, <a href="#bib.bib8" title="" class="ltx_ref">8</a>, <a href="#bib.bib10" title="" class="ltx_ref">10</a>]</cite> needing manual masks, object templates, etc., our approach is fully automatic. We use the SegmentAnything model <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib62" title="" class="ltx_ref">62</a>]</cite> to automatically detect semantic boundaries in the scene to create augmentation masks. We apply augmentations separately to the object under manipulation and the rest of the environment respecting the object and robot boundaries. See <a href="#A1.SS2" title="A.2 Details on Semantic Augmentations ‣ Appendix A Dataset details ‣ RoboAgent: Generalization and Efficiency in Robot Manipulation via Semantic Augmentations and Action Chunking" class="ltx_ref ltx_refmacro_autoref"><span class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">subsection A.2</span></a> for additional details.
We emphasize that our approach toward semantic augmentation is fully automatic and offline. It takes advantage of and is also well poised to continually benefit from rapidly advancing progress in segmentation and in-painting models <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib62" title="" class="ltx_ref">62</a>, <a href="#bib.bib63" title="" class="ltx_ref">63</a>]</cite>. Akin to fields of natural language processing and computer vision, by distilling semantic real-world priors present in internet images/videos into robotics datasets, it provides robot learning a scalable mechanism to benefit from internet-scale data at no extra cost to humans/robots.</p>
</div>
</section>
